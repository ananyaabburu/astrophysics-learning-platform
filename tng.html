Research Tool: IllustrisTNG

IllustrisTNG is a collection of large cosmological computer simulations used to study how galaxies and cosmic structures form and evolve.

Instead of observing only one real galaxy, researchers can use the simulation to follow thousands of modeled galaxies across billions of years of cosmic history.

Type

Cosmological simulation project

Research Areas

Galaxies, dark matter, gas, stars, and black holes

Main Purpose

Model the evolution of the universe

Lesson 1 — What Is IllustrisTNG?

IllustrisTNG stands for Illustris: The Next Generation. It is the successor to the original Illustris simulation project.

The simulation begins with conditions representing the early universe and calculates how matter changes over time under the influence of gravity and other physical processes.

Researchers can examine the simulated universe at different times, compare galaxies, track matter, and test explanations for observed astronomical patterns.

Key Idea: IllustrisTNG is not an image or recording of the real universe. It is a mathematical model generated by a computer using physical laws and assumptions.

Lesson 2 — How Cosmological Simulations Work

A cosmological simulation divides a large region of space into many computational elements. These elements represent matter such as dark matter, gas, stars, and black holes.

The computer repeatedly calculates how these elements move and interact as simulated time advances.

  1. Researchers define starting conditions based on models of the early universe.
  2. Gravity causes matter to gather into filaments, halos, galaxies, and clusters.
  3. Gas falls into dark matter halos and may cool enough to form stars.
  4. Stars, supernovae, and black holes return energy and matter to their surroundings.
  5. Scientists compare the resulting simulated universe with real astronomical observations.

Lesson 3 — Matter Represented in IllustrisTNG

Dark Matter

Dark matter provides much of the gravitational framework in which galaxies form.

It gathers into halos that attract ordinary matter and influence the motion and structure of galaxies.

Gas

Gas can move, heat, cool, and become compressed. Dense, cool gas may form new stars.

Stars

Individual stars are usually not modeled one by one. Instead, one star particle often represents an entire population of stars.

Black Holes

The simulation includes modeled supermassive black holes that can grow by accreting matter and merging.

Magnetic Fields

IllustrisTNG also includes magnetic fields, which can influence gas movement and energetic astrophysical processes.

Heavy Elements

The simulation tracks chemical enrichment as stars produce and release heavier elements into space.

Lesson 4 — Modeling Galaxy Formation

In the early universe, matter was distributed with small variations in density. Regions containing slightly more matter experienced stronger gravity and attracted additional material.

Dark matter gathered into halos. Ordinary gas fell into these halos, became compressed, cooled, and formed stars.

Small early galaxies could merge and grow into larger systems. Over billions of years, the simulation produces spiral, elliptical, irregular, and other galaxy structures.

Comparing Simulations with Observations

Researchers compare simulated galaxies with real data such as:

  • Galaxy sizes
  • Galaxy shapes
  • Stellar masses
  • Star-formation rates
  • Gas content
  • Black-hole masses
  • Galaxy clustering

If the simulation consistently disagrees with observations, scientists may need to improve the model or reconsider some of its assumptions.

Lesson 5 — Feedback from Stars and Black Holes

One of the most important challenges in galaxy simulations is modeling feedback.

Feedback occurs when stars, supernovae, or active black holes release energy or matter into their surroundings.

Stellar Feedback

Young stars produce radiation and stellar winds. Supernova explosions release energy and heavy elements.

These processes can heat or remove gas and reduce the amount available for future star formation.

Black-Hole Feedback

Matter falling toward a supermassive black hole can power an active galactic nucleus.

Energy from this activity can heat gas or push it away from the galaxy, slowing the formation of new stars.

Why feedback matters: Without feedback, simulations may create galaxies that become too massive or form too many stars compared with real galaxies.

Lesson 6 — Working with IllustrisTNG Data

IllustrisTNG produces large datasets describing the simulated universe at many different moments in time.

Researchers can use snapshots, catalogs, visualizations, and programming tools to study the data.

Snapshots

A snapshot stores the state of the simulation at a specific cosmic time. It can include the positions, velocities, temperatures, masses, and other properties of simulated matter.

Group Catalogs

Group catalogs organize matter into structures such as dark matter halos, galaxies, and galaxy clusters.

Merger Trees

Merger trees allow researchers to follow how a galaxy changes over time and identify the smaller systems that merged to form it.

Scientific Questions

A researcher might use TNG data to ask:

  • How does a galaxy's environment affect its shape?
  • When did a particular galaxy stop forming stars?
  • How do black holes and galaxies grow together?
  • How is gas distributed around galaxies?
  • How often do major galaxy mergers occur?

Lesson 7 — Uses and Limitations

Uses

  • Testing theories of galaxy formation
  • Studying dark matter halos
  • Following galaxy mergers
  • Investigating star-formation history
  • Studying feedback from supermassive black holes
  • Comparing simulated observations with telescope data

Limitations

A simulation cannot model every atom, star, or physical process directly. Some processes occur on scales smaller than the simulation can resolve.

Researchers therefore use simplified mathematical rules called subgrid models for processes such as star formation and black-hole feedback.

Simulation results depend on assumptions, numerical methods, resolution, and available computing power.

Important: A successful simulation does not prove that every detail of its model is correct. It shows that the model can reproduce certain observed patterns and can be used to make testable predictions.
