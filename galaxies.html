Module 3: Galaxies

Galaxies are enormous gravitational systems containing stars, stellar remnants, gas, dust, dark matter, and often a supermassive black hole. Studying galaxies helps astronomers understand how matter is organized and how the universe has changed over billions of years.

Difficulty

Prerequisites

Learning Objectives

After completing this module, you should be able to:

  • Explain what components make up a galaxy.

Lesson 1 — What Is a Galaxy?

A galaxy is a gravitationally bound system containing stars, gas, dust, stellar remnants, dark matter, and other objects. Galaxies vary greatly in size. Small dwarf galaxies may contain millions of stars, while giant galaxies may contain trillions.

Visible matter represents only part of a galaxy's total mass. Astronomers infer that most galaxies are surrounded by much larger halos of invisible dark matter. The gravity of this dark matter affects the motion of stars and gas.

Galaxies are not distributed randomly. Many belong to groups, clusters, and even larger structures called superclusters. Between these large concentrations are enormous regions with relatively little matter called cosmic voids.

Lesson 2 — Galaxy Classification

Astronomers classify galaxies mainly by their visible shape, or morphology. Edwin Hubble developed an influential classification system that separated galaxies into elliptical, spiral, barred spiral, and irregular categories.

Spiral Galaxies

Spiral galaxies contain a flattened rotating disk, a central bulge, spiral arms, and a surrounding halo. The spiral arms often contain gas, dust, and regions of active star formation. Young, hot, blue stars make many spiral arms appear bright.

Barred Spiral Galaxies

In a barred spiral galaxy, stars form a bar-shaped structure across the central region. Spiral arms extend from the ends of the bar. The Milky Way is classified as a barred spiral galaxy.

Elliptical Galaxies

Elliptical galaxies range from nearly spherical to strongly elongated. They usually contain less cold gas and dust than spiral galaxies, so they tend to form fewer new stars. Their stellar populations are often older.

Lenticular Galaxies

Lenticular galaxies have a disk and central bulge but lack prominent spiral arms. They are sometimes described as an intermediate form between spiral and elliptical galaxies.

Irregular Galaxies

Irregular galaxies do not have a clear spiral or elliptical structure. Their unusual shapes may result from gravitational interactions, mergers, or strong episodes of star formation.

Lesson 3 — The Milky Way

The Milky Way is the galaxy containing the Solar System. It is a barred spiral galaxy with a disk roughly 100,000 light-years across. The exact size depends on how astronomers define the faint outer regions and dark matter halo.

Galactic Disk

The disk contains most of the Milky Way's gas, dust, and spiral structure. It also contains many young stars and star-forming regions. The Solar System lies within the disk in a smaller feature called the Orion Spur.

Central Bulge and Bar

The central region contains a dense concentration of stars and a bar-shaped structure. At the center is Sagittarius A*, a supermassive black hole with a mass of roughly four million Suns.

Stellar Halo

The stellar halo surrounds the disk and contains old stars and globular clusters. Globular clusters are tightly packed groups containing thousands to millions of stars.

Dark Matter Halo

A much larger dark matter halo surrounds the visible galaxy. Although dark matter does not emit light, its gravity strongly influences the motion of stars, gas, and satellite galaxies.

Lesson 4 — Dark Matter and Galaxy Rotation

Astronomers can estimate a galaxy's mass by measuring how quickly stars and gas orbit its center. If most mass were concentrated in the visible central regions, orbital speeds should decrease significantly at large distances.

Instead, observations show that stars and gas in the outer regions of many galaxies continue orbiting at unexpectedly high speeds. These measurements create nearly flat galaxy rotation curves.

One major explanation is that galaxies are surrounded by large halos of dark matter. This invisible material provides additional gravity without producing detectable light.

What is dark matter?

Dark matter is a proposed form of matter that interacts gravitationally but does not emit, absorb, or reflect enough electromagnetic radiation to be directly observed. Its exact particle nature remains unknown.

Other evidence for dark matter includes gravitational lensing, galaxy-cluster motion, the cosmic microwave background, and the growth of large-scale structure.

Lesson 5 — Galaxy Formation and Evolution

Galaxies began forming from small density variations in the early universe. Regions containing slightly more matter than average experienced stronger gravity and attracted additional matter.

Dark matter formed gravitational structures into which ordinary gas could fall. As gas cooled and became denser, it formed stars and early galaxies.

Star Formation and Feedback

Galaxy evolution depends on a balance between gas entering the galaxy, gas forming stars, and energy being returned to the surrounding environment. Supernovae, stellar winds, and active galactic nuclei can heat or remove gas.

These feedback processes can reduce future star formation. Without feedback, computer simulations often predict galaxies that form too many stars too quickly.

Environmental Effects

A galaxy's environment can affect its evolution. In dense galaxy clusters, interactions with hot gas and neighboring galaxies can remove a galaxy's cold gas or alter its shape.

Lesson 6 — Galaxy Collisions and Mergers

Galaxies can collide because gravity changes their paths. Although the word collision sounds destructive, individual stars usually do not collide because the distances between stars are enormous.

Gas clouds can interact more directly. These interactions may compress gas and trigger intense bursts of star formation. Tidal forces can stretch galaxies and create long tails of stars and gas.

During a major merger, two galaxies may combine into one larger system. Mergers can transform spiral galaxies, change stellar orbits, fuel central black holes, and sometimes produce elliptical galaxies.

Lesson 7 — Active Galaxies and Quasars

Some galaxies contain extremely bright central regions called active galactic nuclei, or AGN. Their energy comes from matter falling toward a supermassive black hole.

Gas in the accretion disk becomes extremely hot and releases enormous amounts of radiation before crossing the event horizon. In some cases, the central region can outshine the combined starlight of the entire galaxy.

Quasars

Quasars are especially luminous active galactic nuclei. Because many quasars are extremely distant, astronomers observe them as they existed billions of years ago.

Radio Galaxies and Jets

Some active galaxies produce powerful relativistic jets. Charged particles moving through magnetic fields emit radiation, creating enormous radio-emitting structures extending far beyond the visible galaxy.

Not every galaxy with a supermassive black hole is currently active. The black hole must have a sufficient supply of nearby material to produce a luminous accretion disk.

Real Research Connections

  • Hubble Space Telescope: Studies galaxy morphology, collisions, star formation, and distant galaxies.
  • James Webb Space Telescope: Observes early galaxies in infrared light and investigates how the first galaxies formed.
  • ESA Gaia Mission: Maps the positions and motions of stars throughout the Milky Way.
  • IllustrisTNG: Uses large computer simulations to model galaxy formation, dark matter, gas, stars, and black holes.
  • Sloan Digital Sky Survey: Collects images and spectra for millions of astronomical objects, including galaxies.
